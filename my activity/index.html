What phishing actually looks like

Links aren't the whole story — most phishing shows up as a full email or text first. Here's a side-by-side of a fake and a real one.

Phishing example
From: PayPaI Security <alert@paypal-secure-login.verify-account.xyz> Subject: Your account has been limited — action required

Dear Customer,

We detected unusual activity on your account. Your access has been temporarily limited. To avoid permanent suspension, verify your identity within 24 hours.

Confirm My Account →

Failure to act immediately will result in permanent account closure.

— PayPal Account Review Team

  • Generic greeting — "Dear Customer," not your real name
  • Sender domain mimics the brand but isn't the real one
  • Manufactured urgency and a countdown ("24 hours")
  • Threatens a consequence (account closure) to rush you
  • Pushes you to click instead of logging in directly
Legit example
From: PayPal <service@paypal.com> Subject: A new device signed in to your account

Hi Alex,

We noticed a new sign-in to your account from a Windows device in Manila, Philippines on Aug 11, 2026.

If this was you, no action is needed. If you don't recognize this activity, we recommend changing your password from the app or by typing paypal.com directly into your browser.

— The PayPal Team

  • Addresses you by name, not a generic label
  • Sender address matches the real company domain
  • States facts (device, location, date) instead of vague threats
  • No countdown, no "act now or else"
  • Tells you to type the address yourself rather than click a link
